**Top 3**

- **Walmart's Q3 FY2027 results (reported 19 November) show marketplace growth holding above 45% for a third straight quarter, alongside profitable monetisation across advertising and membership.** US e-commerce sales rose 23% and global e-commerce rose 17% (Sam's Club US e-commerce +30%); within that, US marketplace and fulfilment-services net sales rose 49% — fashion categories grew over 40%, and 100 new beauty brands drove 75% of that category's sales growth. CFO John David Rainey said US e-commerce incremental margins "exceeded 12%" this quarter and that the business is "pretty close to break-even without advertising." US advertising revenue rose 37% (Walmart Connect +36%) and global membership-fee revenue rose 17%. The new combined Walmart+/Sam's Club Plus membership bundle, launched 20 October at $120 a year, is already showing effect: chief growth officer Seth Dallaire said linked, upgraded or newly-bundled members are "spending even more than the cohort of dual members" seen before launch, and a Walmart US executive said new bundled members raised their online spend with Walmart by more than 80% versus before. Walmart's Sparky AI shopping assistant — built on the Agent Gateway framework for third-party AI agents announced in October — saw usage up 129% year-on-year with baskets up more than 40%; CEO John Furner said "agents are beginning to play a bigger role in more shopping journeys, and we believe Walmart is well-positioned." Walmart raised full-year guidance (enterprise sales growth to 4.9–5.2%, from 4–5%; adjusted EPS to $2.84–$2.89, from $2.80–$2.87). *Why it matters: three consecutive quarters of marketplace growth above 45% at a retailer Walmart's size is the clearest evidence yet that a large, commission-style marketplace can keep compounding rather than plateauing — and pairing that volume growth with genuinely profitable monetisation (advertising, membership, narrowing e-commerce losses) is exactly the combination a commission-model B2B platform should want to be able to point to.*

- **The European Commission proposed a Digital Fairness Act on 11 November — missed at the time, and still the most directly marketplace-relevant regulatory move this digest has tracked in weeks.** The proposal would replace the EU's Unfair Commercial Practices Directive with a directly-applicable Regulation covering, among other things: an express ban and blacklist of "dark patterns" (including false scarcity claims), drip pricing and misleading price comparisons, restrictions on profiling-based advertising or pricing aimed at minors or vulnerable consumers, mandatory disclosure for influencer marketing, a right for adult users to disable addictive design features (autoplay, unprompted notifications, streaks), clearer subscription cancellation rights, and — specifically relevant to agentic commerce — a requirement that consumers using AI shopping agents control their own purchase-confirmation settings, with traders required to offer human assistance on request. It applies explicitly to "B2C online platforms and marketplaces," alongside retailers, travel/ticketing firms, app developers and influencers, and to non-EU businesses targeting EU consumers. Large firms face fines of up to 6% of global annual turnover for substantive breaches, enforced directly by the Commission; micro, small and medium-sized traders are excluded from that direct-enforcement regime. The proposal now goes to the European Parliament and Council, where this kind of negotiation typically takes around 17 months on average, and would apply 12 months after it formally enters into force — so nothing changes immediately, but the direction is set. *Why it matters: this is a more specific, more marketplace-targeted instrument than the DMA's gatekeeper-only scope — it would put dark-pattern design, drip pricing and AI-agent purchase consent under a single binding EU statute that applies to ordinary marketplaces, not just the largest platforms. Worth flagging to Booker's own legal/compliance function now, on the long runway ahead, given the UK's CMA has already pursued dark-pattern and subscription-trap enforcement under existing consumer law independent of this EU proposal.*

- **Two separate eBay-related trust and governance stories, both missed at the time, surfaced on 13 November: a confirmed data breach at eBay-owned Qoo10, and a new lawsuit from eBay's own insurer over its 2026 cyberstalking settlement.** Qoo10 — the Japanese marketplace eBay Japan acquired in 2018, with more than 30 million registered members — disclosed that an outside party gained unauthorised access to its systems on 6 November and viewed some customer data, including names, email addresses, phone numbers, birth dates, home and delivery addresses, and geographic coordinates tied to those addresses; Qoo10 says it has found no evidence payment details or passwords were exposed, but has not disclosed how many accounts were affected or how the access occurred. Separately, ACE Property & Casualty Insurance filed suit against eBay and former executives including ex-CEO Devin Wenig in the US District Court for the Northern District of California (filed 12 November), arguing it never consented to eBay's $55.7m settlement of the 2019 Steiner cyberstalking case (in which eBay admitted an "unacceptable tone from the top" after staff harassed a couple who had criticised the company in a newsletter) and that the underlying conduct was intentional, and therefore excluded from its coverage. *Why it matters: both are reminders that a marketplace's trust and legal exposure doesn't expire — a near-decade-old harassment scandal is still generating fresh litigation in 2026, and a 2018 acquisition is still generating fresh breach disclosures in 2026. Worth treating supplier- and buyer-trust incidents as standing reputational liabilities to manage for years, not closed incidents, as Booker Marketplace's own data footprint and executive accountability structures scale.*

- Texas's proposed repeal of its marketplace-fee "data processing" tax (Comptroller Don Huffines's Rule 3.330 amendment) has still not been formally adopted as of 20 November — its public-comment period closed 15 November, as previously flagged, but no Texas Register adoption date has been published yet, so sellers must keep paying the existing tax in the meantime.

- Amazon's "Black Friday Week" runs 19–30 November this year, with the retailer promising deals dropping twice on both Black Friday and Cyber Monday — an earlier and longer promotional window than prior years, continuing the trend this digest has tracked of holiday sales events creeping earlier into the calendar.

**B2B relevance for Booker Marketplace**

- Walmart's three-straight-quarter run of marketplace growth above 45%, paired with narrowing e-commerce losses and strong advertising/membership monetisation, is a concrete benchmark worth returning to: it shows a commission-style marketplace model scaling profitably over a sustained period, not just in a single strong quarter. Worth checking how Booker's own marketplace revenue mix — commission versus any advertising, membership or data-service layers — compares to that combination as a target shape, not just a GMV number.

- The EU's Digital Fairness Act is worth a heads-up to Booker's legal and compliance teams now, even on its long legislative runway: its dark-pattern ban, drip-pricing restrictions and AI-shopping-agent purchase-consent requirement all land squarely on marketplace checkout and pricing-display design. The UK is not bound by an EU directive, but the CMA's own existing consumer-law enforcement on dark patterns and subscription traps means a comparable standard is already live domestically — worth checking Booker Marketplace's own checkout flow and any AI-agent ordering path against both now, rather than waiting for either regime to bite.

- The Qoo10 breach and the ACE/eBay litigation are both worth banking as a governance lesson this digest keeps returning to: data-breach and executive-conduct risk doesn't retire when an incident is resolved or an acquisition closes — both resurfaced years later. Worth treating Booker's own supplier- and buyer-data safeguards, and any legacy conduct exposure, as a standing watch item rather than a one-time compliance exercise.
