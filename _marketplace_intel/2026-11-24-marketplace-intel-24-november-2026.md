*Covering 20–24 November 2026, since the last digest, plus two items that broke just before that window but were missed: Amazon Business's UK launch of Prime Business Rewards (19 November) and a Reuters report that the US Department of Justice will oversee the DoorDash–Instacart merger review (16 November). UK wholesale/B2B marketplace competitor news was, for once, not entirely thin this window — the Prime Business Rewards launch is a genuine, dated Amazon Business UK item — but nothing dated from Bestway, Nisa, Musgrave or Costco UK turned up, so that part of the picture stays short rather than padded.*

**Top 3**

- **Etsy rewrote its AI-training terms within four weeks of publishing them, after seller backlash and an apology from its CEO (revised terms published 23 November, effective 16 December).** Etsy's 26 October Terms of Use update had explicitly authorised Etsy and its partners to use seller-uploaded content — listings, photos, reviews, community posts — to "develop, train, test and improve" AI systems, with no clear opt-out for US sellers; coverage (including the New York Times from 20 November) and threats from sellers to close their shops followed, tied partly to Etsy's AI shopping partnerships with OpenAI, Google and Microsoft. The 23 November revision keeps the same underlying permission in Section 5(C) but reframes it around specific purposes (buyer search, seller tools, customer support, fraud prevention) and adds an explicit new assurance: "We won't use Your Content to train AI models designed to replace your creativity or to create copycat versions of the items you sell on Etsy." CEO Kruti Patel Goyal told sellers directly: "I acknowledge that we should've given you more context on what the updates meant for your creative content when we shared them." Notably, the revision quietly dropped an earlier reference to "applicable opt-out rights described in our Privacy Policy" without explanation, and no specific US opt-out mechanism has been identified by outside reviewers; EU/UK sellers may have narrower objection rights under data-protection law. *Why it matters: this is a live, fast-moving example of exactly the governance risk this digest has flagged repeatedly (eBay's AI-training disclosure update, Meta's contested advertiser terms) — a platform writing broad AI-training rights into terms sellers can't negotiate, getting called out publicly, and having to walk part of it back within weeks. The copycat-specific carve-out is a new, more concrete middle ground worth studying: narrower than a blanket ban, more reassuring than a blanket permission.*

- **Depop's new US Terms of Service, taking effect 1 January 2027, explicitly bans AI "buy-for-me" shopping agents and adds mandatory arbitration with a class-action waiver — the opposite direction from Walmart's consent-based Agent Gateway model this digest covered last month.** The new terms (US users only; published in stages from 18 November, updated 23 November) bar "any robot, spider, scraper … or other automated means (including, without limitation, buy-for-me agents, LLM-driven bots …)" from using Depop without prior express permission, in wording that closely tracks eBay's own early-2026 agent ban — notable since eBay completed its $1.4bn Depop acquisition in July. Alongside the agent ban, Depop adds a mandatory-arbitration clause with a 60-day informal dispute process, a class/collective-action waiver, and a batch-arbitration mechanism for 25-or-more similar claims; users can opt out only by mailed notice to Depop's Litigation Department within a tight, and inconsistently stated, window around the effective date. The update also removes existing protections — a 45-day notice period and 30-day cure window before account termination — in favour of termination "at any time for any reason, at our sole discretion," and gives Depop new authority to place holds on seller funds where it "reasonably believes" risk management requires it. *Why it matters: this lands only weeks after the EU's proposed Digital Fairness Act flagged a requirement that consumers using AI shopping agents keep control of their own purchase-confirmation settings, and directly contrasts with Walmart's sanctioned, consent-and-spend-cap model for agentic buying. Two different marketplaces are now answering "how should an AI agent interact with our platform" in opposite ways — block outright, or build a governed path — and the gap between those answers is becoming a real strategic choice, not just a policy footnote.*

- **Amazon Business launched "Prime Business Rewards" in the UK, a points-back loyalty scheme aimed squarely at sole traders and small businesses (19 November, missed by the last digest).** Members earn points (100 points = £1) on Amazon Business purchases, redeemable on future orders and expiring after 12 months; Duo and Basic plans earn up to 2% back on eligible Amazon Private Brands purchases, rising to 4% on the Small plan. Amy Worth, UK Director and GM of Amazon Business, said the offer is "built for the businesses where the owner is also the buyer." A Black Friday Week (19–30 November) promotion layers on top: eligible new members on qualifying plans can earn up to 50% back on eligible orders up to £40, and offers can be stacked with extra rewards for adding team members, enabling Guided Buying policies, or activating Subscribe & Save. Amazon Business says it has passed $60bn in annualised global gross sales and now serves more than 11 million organisations. *Why it matters: this is Amazon Business building a cashback-style loyalty layer specifically for the smallest, most price-sensitive UK business buyers — the segment a wholesale commission marketplace like Booker also depends on — using recurring-order and procurement-policy features (Subscribe & Save, Guided Buying) as additional earning triggers rather than just a flat discount. Worth watching whether a comparable points-back or stacked-offer mechanic could help Booker Marketplace retain small independent buyers, rather than competing on headline price alone.*

**Wider roundup**

- A Reuters report (16 November, via sources, reported further by PYMNTS/CPI on 17 November and missed by the last digest) says the US Department of Justice, not the FTC, will oversee the antitrust review of DoorDash's $17bn agreed acquisition of Instacart — though a separate Daily Record report says the FTC "traditionally handles" grocery-sector mergers, so which agency actually leads remains unconfirmed by any government statement. Neither company has commented; the DOJ has not addressed the matter publicly. DoorDash owes Instacart a $1.25bn break fee if the deal fails to clear antitrust review. Separately, the FTC's 28 October personalised-pricing information orders to DoorDash, Instacart, Walmart and six others reportedly gave each company 45 days to respond (implying early-to-mid December), though the orders are a study, not an allegation of wrongdoing, and Walmart and Instacart have both denied using personalised pricing.

- eBay and Whatnot are going head-to-head on Black Friday seller economics: both are offering 0% seller commissions over the holiday weekend, with Whatnot extending its fee discount across four days and offering up to $1m in giveaways to small sellers.

- Ron Jaiven, eBay's North America GM, is moving to lead TCGplayer (eBay's trading-card subsidiary), which has passed $1bn in GMV; he replaces former CEO Rob Bigler after a little over a year running eBay's North American marketplace.

- Poshmark is continuing to pull back from social-selling features in its Q4 updates — removing follower, sharing and party notifications from sellers' newsfeeds — in favour of AI-powered shopping tools and buyer discovery; some previously promised seller-facing changes remain on hold.

- Vinted is expanding third-party display advertising across its US marketplace, with shoppers reporting ads between listings and in messages — including ads for rival marketplace Depop appearing inside Vinted's own app.

- Mercari is expanding free shipping to orders of $20 or more in toys, collectibles and gaming, following management comments that rivals' aggressive shipping discounts are hurting sales — a continuation of the stalled-US-growth story (GMV growth of just 0.3%) this digest flagged on 17 November.

- Depop launched an NYC package drop-off service through participating local businesses, part of the shared US shipping network eBay is building across both platforms — a concrete, geography-specific rollout of the Shipping-as-a-Service build this digest covered in detail on 17 November.

- Texas's proposed repeal of its marketplace-fee "data processing" tax (Rule 3.330) remains unresolved: its public-comment period closed 15 November, but no Texas Register adoption notice has been published as this digest was compiled, so sellers must keep paying the existing tax for now.

- No new dated developments surfaced this window in the FTC v Amazon ad-auction pricing suit, the FTC's separate Amazon monopolisation case, the EU DMA Meta/Facebook Marketplace gatekeeper case, the EU Commission's Amazon price-parity probe, the EU's proposed Digital Fairness Act, France's fast-fashion levy, the Qoo10 data breach, or the ACE/eBay insurance litigation — those all remain as previously recorded.

**B2B relevance for Booker Marketplace**

- Etsy's four-week reversal on its AI-training terms, complete with a direct CEO apology, is the clearest governance lesson this digest has carried on the subject: publishing broad, unexplained AI-data-use rights without a clear opt-out invites exactly the kind of seller backlash that forces an embarrassing, fast rewrite. Worth treating Booker's own eventual supplier-data-and-AI policy as something to get right — with a clear, specific, well-communicated scope — before publishing, not after a reaction forces changes.

- Depop's outright ban on AI buy-for-me agents, set against Walmart's consent-and-spend-cap Agent Gateway model, crystallises a real strategic fork this digest has flagged building for months: block AI agents outright, or build a governed path for them (identity, consent, spend limits, revocable access). Worth deciding deliberately which model fits Booker Marketplace's own buyer base — sole traders and small retailers are exactly the segment most likely to start using AI procurement assistants — rather than defaulting to one position by inertia.

- Amazon Business's Prime Business Rewards is the most directly transferable idea this week: a simple points-back loyalty layer, stacked with bonus triggers tied to recurring-order and procurement-policy features, aimed at the same owner-operator small-business buyer segment Booker Marketplace serves. Worth asking whether a comparable loyalty or stacked-offer mechanic could help retain Booker's own smaller independent buyers against exactly this kind of Amazon Business push.

- The DoorDash–Instacart deal's drifting, still-unconfirmed antitrust review (DOJ or FTC, nobody will yet say) is a reminder that marketplace-on-marketplace consolidation keeps drawing sustained regulatory attention even well after a deal is announced — relevant background as commission-model platforms of all sizes continue to consolidate.
