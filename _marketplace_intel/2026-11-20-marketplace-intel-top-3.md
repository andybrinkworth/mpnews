- **eBay is building "Shipping as a Service" and "Authentication as a Service" to sell to rival marketplaces, starting with Depop (reported 16 November).** Job postings for a Bengaluru-based Product Manager role and a Toronto engineering role describe eBay extending its own shipping and authenticity-verification infrastructure beyond eBay.com to outside platforms; Depop is named as the first marketplace to get the authentication product, with Tise, TCGplayer, Goldin and the eBay-owned Japanese marketplace Qoo10 flagged as other likely beneficiaries. Andres Gonzalez, eBay's VP and GM for Global Shipping & Authentication, called both areas "must-win priorities"; one posting states plainly, "We're expanding these capabilities beyond eBay, starting with Depop." eBay's Authenticity Guarantee programme passed 20 million cumulative items inspected as of July 2026, and currently charges US buyers an optional $20 verification fee on items priced $200–$499.99 (rising to $80 for watches priced $500–$1,999.99), with a €10 equivalent in Germany for designer items over €100. The move echoes, almost exactly, the pitch GameStop's Ryan Cohen made in his rejected $56bn takeover bid in May — using GameStop's roughly 1,600 US stores as a physical shipping and authentication network for eBay — except eBay is now building the service itself rather than through GameStop, which still holds roughly a 9.8% stake. Why it matters: this is the clearest sign yet that a marketplace's trust-and-logistics infrastructure, not just its storefront, can become a product sold to competitors — a genuine marketplace-as-a-service move. For a commission-model B2B platform, it raises a direct question: which parts of Booker Marketplace's own infrastructure (supplier verification, depot network, delivery) might eventually be worth selling as a service to others, rather than only using internally?

- **Reuters' exclusive report on Amazon's internal "World Product Graph" plan is the standout item this digest missed on its original publication date — worth covering properly now (first published 13 November, updated 14 November).** Citing an internal six-page planning document and people familiar with it, Reuters (byline Greg Bensinger) reports Amazon is planning to build a database of "virtually every item for sale on Earth, whether Amazon stocks it or not," combining data contributed directly by brands and retailers (Coca-Cola and Bayer have signed on; P&G has held a trial summit) with Amazon's own crawling of other retailers' sites — Reuters could not establish how the crawling works or whether permission is sought. The project, owned by Amazon's Selection & Catalog Systems unit under VP Robert Tekiela, is explicitly aimed at fixing Rufus (renamed Alexa for Shopping in May), which the internal document admits gives "many … factually wrong or incomplete" answers despite roughly 83 million weekly searches, and at keeping shoppers from leaving Amazon to research products elsewhere — countering AI shopping agents such as Meta's Muse (blocked from Amazon's site in September, after passing 10 million US/Canada users), Perplexity (which Amazon is suing) and xAI's Grok. Amazon says the document is an outdated, incomplete "brainstorming" artefact and that Alexa for Shopping is "roughly 97% accurate globally"; the document's own target launch date of 1 October 2027 is, per Reuters, unconfirmed as realistic. Why it matters: this is a dominant marketplace explicitly trying to own product data outside its own catalogue so it isn't disintermediated by AI shopping agents — a sharper, more concrete version of the "who controls product discovery" question this digest has tracked since September. For Booker Marketplace, it's a reminder that catalogue completeness and data quality are becoming a competitive moat in their own right, not just a UX nicety.

- **Faire, the B2B wholesale marketplace this digest's own background section once described as a "grew too fast and nearly broke the company" cautionary tale, now says it is eyeing a possible IPO within the next 12 months (Fortune, 2 November, missed at the time).** CFO Jason Lee is preparing the company for a listing, though with no committed timeline. Faire's most recent valuation, set in a $100m tender offer in November 2025, was $5.2bn — well down from its $12.6bn peak in May 2022, but a real recovery from the depths of its 2022 reset. The company posted $750m in annualised revenue last quarter, up 49% year-on-year, and reported its first-ever EBITDA breakeven. CEO Max Rhodes described the 2021–22 period as a "fog of crazy valuations" during which Faire "got a little addicted to vanity metrics to pump growth," signed on brands too fast, and suffered retailer churn and high marketing costs as a result; the recovery has come from deliberately shrinking the brand base, adding retention technology, and focusing on reorder rates rather than headline sign-up volume, which Rhodes says have "gone way up" as retailers return for products that actually sell. Faire now serves roughly 500,000 retailers and 100,000 brands. Why it matters: this is a direct, dated update to a story this digest has returned to more than once as a B2B marketplace cautionary tale — and the lesson from the recovery is as relevant as the lesson from the near-failure. Faire's shift from vanity growth metrics to reorder-rate and retailer-retention discipline is a concrete, B2B-specific precedent worth weighing against however Booker Marketplace currently measures its own supplier and buyer growth.
