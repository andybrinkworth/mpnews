- **DoorDash agreed to acquire Instacart for roughly $17bn, consolidating the two largest US delivery marketplaces (29 Oct; missed by the last digest).** The DoorDash- and Instacart-board-approved deal — reported at $65 per Instacart share in a cash-and-stock structure — would see DoorDash keep Instacart's brand, app and enterprise technology (the retailer-facing online-ordering and advertising tools Instacart sells to grocers) while the two companies operate separately until closing, expected toward the end of 2027 or in the first half of 2028. Instacart cancelled its 5 November earnings call and suspended its financial guidance the same day. DoorDash CEO Tony Xu framed the deal as pairing DoorDash's restaurant-delivery strength with Instacart's grocery reach; Instacart CEO Chris Rogers called it a way to build "the operating system for grocery." Analysts (Emarketer's Zak Stambor) expect the grocery overlap to draw real antitrust scrutiny, and the FTC already has an open study into whether DoorDash and Instacart use individual shoppers' data to set prices and driver wages; 2025 all-delivery market-share data from YipitData puts DoorDash at 65.1% and Instacart at 9.1% (Uber Eats 25.8%), though that figure spans restaurant and retail delivery, not grocery alone, and DoorDash points to Walmart, Amazon, Target and Gopuff as grocery-delivery competitors the deal would still leave standing. Why it matters: this is the biggest marketplace-on-marketplace consolidation this digest has tracked all year, and it lands squarely on top of a thread already running here — DoorDash's own pivot into a general retail marketplace with Macy's, Anthropologie and VF Corp brands plus paid returns handling (covered 6 October). The deal pairs that general-retail ambition with Instacart's retailer-facing ordering and advertising technology, which is exactly the "marketplace plus ads" bundle a commission platform like Booker is itself building — and a reminder that regulators are now scrutinising shopper-data concentration and algorithmic pricing/wage-setting in grocery and retail delivery, not just classic Amazon-style antitrust turf.

- **The EU's Union Handling Fee and mandatory customs Product Identifier rule, both flagged repeatedly in this digest since early October, took effect on 1 November 2026.** The Union Handling Fee adds €2 per item (on top of the existing €3 duty) to parcels entering the EU from outside it, with no value threshold, no refund even if goods are faulty or returned, and — per Irish Revenue's own guidance — intended to be borne by the platforms and businesses bringing the packages in rather than charged directly to shoppers. Alongside it, the mandatory Merchant Product Identifier requirement now applies to all EU customs declarations regardless of parcel value, needing a standardised code (GTIN/EAN/UPC/ISBN) where one exists and a manufacturer-assigned identifier otherwise — the gap this digest flagged on 13 October for vintage, handmade and long-tail goods with no manufacturer-level identifier at all. Why it matters: these are the first entries where previously-flagged EU compliance deadlines have actually gone live rather than remaining pending — worth watching the next few digests for real seller and buyer friction (price rises, customs delays, blocked shipments for undocumented goods) now that both rules are in force rather than theoretical.

- **Amazon's $1m liability-insurance mandate for "enhanced safety" seller categories, flagged back on 7 September, took effect on 2 November 2026 — landing in the middle of Amazon's already-stacked peak-season fee calendar.** Sellers in the affected categories now need $1m commercial liability cover regardless of sales volume (replacing the old $10k/month sales threshold that previously exempted smaller sellers), and mainland China-based sellers must obtain it through Amazon's own Insurance Accelerator product rather than an outside insurer. It lands three weeks after FBA peak fees began (15 October) and in the middle of Amazon's 25 October–16 January shipping peak surcharges, both already recorded in this digest's background section. Why it matters: another example of a previously-announced Amazon seller cost landing exactly when sellers can least absorb a new compliance bill — and a reminder that Amazon continues to pair safety-driven rule-tightening with a push toward its own in-house insurance product, a combination other marketplaces have tended to copy once Amazon sets the precedent.
